## Why go above ground

Burial is the default, but above-ground construction is chosen where:

- the line is **hot**, and burial would mean large compressive stress, upheaval buckling risk and big anchor blocks,
- the ground is **rock** or **sabkha**, where trenching is slow or the soil is highly corrosive,
- the line is **short** and inside an operating area, such as flowlines in an oil field,
- **inspection and maintenance access** is valued over protection,
- the ground is unstable (permafrost, active faults) and the pipe must be isolated from it.

The price is exposure: to vehicles, sun, sand, fire and tampering. Above-ground lines also block access across the corridor unless crossings are provided.

## Restrained or free

Module M2 showed that a buried pipe held by soil develops stress when heated. An above-ground pipe is designed the opposite way: it is **allowed to grow**, and the growth is absorbed by bending in loops, offsets or changes of direction.

Free thermal growth of a length $L$:

$$
\delta = \alpha \, \Delta T \, L
$$

For steel, that is about 1.2 mm per metre for every 100 °C. A 300 m section heated by 60 °C grows 210 mm. The temperature range must include the coldest winter night with the line empty and the hottest condition, which in the desert includes **solar heating** of an empty or stagnant line to 80 °C or more.

## Anchors, guides and supports

The line is divided into sections by **anchors**. Between two anchors the pipe expands toward a loop or offset placed between them.

- **Anchors** fix the pipe in all directions. They carry the loop's spring force and friction from the supports, which is far less than the full restraint force of a buried line.
- **Guides** allow axial sliding but prevent the pipe moving sideways or lifting off, so that growth goes where the designer intends and the line cannot buckle laterally.
- **Sliding supports** carry weight and let the pipe move. Low-friction pads (PTFE on stainless steel) reduce the friction force on anchors. Support height keeps the pipe clear of the ground, drifting sand and standing water.
- **Wear pads or saddles** welded or clamped to the pipe protect it and its coating where it rests on a support.

## Absorbing the growth

- **Expansion loop:** a U-shaped offset. The legs perpendicular to the line bend as the pipe grows into the loop.
- **Z-bend or L-bend (offset):** a change of direction used as flexibility.
- **Zig-zag or snaked lay:** the whole line laid in a gentle wave, so growth is taken up by slight changes in curvature.

A first estimate of the leg length needed uses the guided-cantilever formula:

$$
L_{leg} = \sqrt{\frac{3 \, E \, D \, \delta}{S_A}}
$$

where $\delta$ is the movement the leg absorbs and $S_A$ is the allowable expansion stress range. Larger diameter and more movement both need longer legs.

## Support spacing

Support span is limited by:

- **bending stress** in the pipe between supports,
- **deflection (sag)**, which must be small enough for liquid lines to drain and to avoid ponding of water in gas lines,
- **local stress at the support**, where the pipe wall bears on the saddle: thin-wall large-diameter pipe may need a wider saddle or reinforcing pad,
- **natural frequency**, kept high enough to avoid wind-induced vibration.

Typical spans range from about 6 m for small pipe to 20 m or more for large diameters.

## Hazards of exposed pipe

- **Vehicle impact.** Barriers, berms or burial at road crossings.
- **External corrosion** from the atmosphere, and especially at supports, where water and salt-laden sand collect in the crevice between pipe and support.
- **Sand accumulation.** Drifting sand buries low pipe, which is then partly restrained, partly corroding and impossible to inspect. Supports are raised, and sand is cleared.
- **Solar heating and UV,** which affect temperature range and coatings.
- **Fire exposure** in process areas.
- **Wind and flood loads** on spans and at wadi crossings.
- **Surge and slug forces** at bends, which can shift an unrestrained line off its supports.

Above-ground pipe is painted or coated for atmospheric service. It is not cathodically protected, since CP needs an electrolyte, so the transition where a line goes below ground needs both systems and an isolation joint.

<Callout type="field">
The most common corrosion on an above-ground line is not on the visible top. It is at the 6 o'clock position where the pipe sits on its supports. Inspect there, by lifting the pipe or with guided-wave ultrasonics.
</Callout>

<Worked title="Expansion loop for an NPS 24 above-ground line">

**Given:** OD 610 mm, wall 12.7 mm, X65, anchors 300 m apart, temperature range 60 °C.

**Growth:** 11.7 × 10⁻⁶ × 60 × 300 000 = **211 mm**.

**Loop at mid-length:** each leg absorbs half, 105 mm.

**Allowable expansion stress range:** 0.72 × 450 = 324 MPa.

$$
L_{leg} = \sqrt{\frac{3 \times 207\,000 \times 610 \times 105}{324}} = 11\,100 \text{ mm} = 11.1 \text{ m}
$$

**For comparison:** holding the same pipe fully restrained would take E·α·ΔT·A = 145 MPa × 23 831 mm² = **3460 kN** at each anchor. With the loop, the anchors carry only support friction and the loop's bending reaction, typically a few percent of that.

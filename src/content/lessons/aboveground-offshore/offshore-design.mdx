## What changes offshore

The steel, the welds and the pressure are the same. The environment is not.

| Aspect | Onshore buried | Offshore |
|---|---|---|
| Governing wall thickness | Internal pressure, location class | Often installation bending, collapse or stability |
| External pressure | Negligible | Up to hundreds of bar in deep water |
| Restraint | Soil | Self-weight and seabed friction, or trenching and burial |
| Main external hazards | Excavators | Anchors, trawl gear, dropped objects |
| Corrosion protection | Coating plus impressed current CP | Coating plus bracelet sacrificial anodes |
| Construction | Many crews along the route | One lay vessel |
| Access for repair | Excavate | Divers or remotely operated vehicles |
| Cost of a mistake | High | Very high |

## Limit-state design

Onshore codes mostly limit **stress** to a fraction of yield. The main offshore code, DNV-ST-F101, uses **limit-state design**: each way the pipe could fail is checked separately, with partial safety factors on loads and resistance that depend on the consequence of failure (the safety class).

The limit states include:

- **Pressure containment (burst).**
- **Collapse** under external pressure, and **propagating buckling**, where a local collapse runs along the pipe (stopped by buckle arrestors).
- **Local buckling** under combined bending, axial force and pressure.
- **Global buckling:** upheaval or lateral.
- **Fatigue** from waves, vortex-induced vibration and installation.
- **Fracture** of girth welds under installation strain.
- **On-bottom stability.**

Because installation often bends the pipe beyond yield, strain-based criteria are routine offshore.

## Wall thickness

Three checks usually compete:

1. **Internal pressure** (burst), as onshore.
2. **External pressure collapse** with the pipe empty at maximum water depth (module M2).
3. **Combined loading during installation,** in the overbend and sagbend of the lay.

In deep water, collapse and installation commonly govern, and D/t is lower than for an equivalent onshore line.

## On-bottom stability

A pipe lying on the seabed is pushed by currents and wave-induced water motion. Water flowing over it creates **drag** (horizontal), **lift** (upward) and, in waves, **inertia** forces. It must be heavy enough that seabed friction resists sliding even with lift reducing its effective weight:

$$
W_s \geq \gamma \left( \frac{F_D + F_I}{\mu} + F_L \right)
$$

where $W_s$ is the submerged weight per metre and $\mu$ the friction coefficient between pipe and seabed.

Weight is added with **concrete weight coating**. Where weight alone would be excessive, the pipe is trenched, buried or covered with rock.

Try this: with no concrete the empty pipe floats. Add concrete until it is stable at 1 m/s, then double the velocity. Forces rise with velocity squared, so the weight needed quadruples. Now switch to clay and see why trenching is preferred on low-friction seabeds.

## Other design topics

- **Free spans.** Uneven seabed leaves the pipe unsupported. Spans are checked for static stress and vortex-induced vibration (module M2), and corrected with grout bags, rock or trenching.
- **Global buckling.** Hot subsea lines lying on the seabed buckle laterally. Design manages this with planned buckle sites.
- **Protection.** Burial or rock cover in fishing and shipping areas; concrete coating against trawl boards; exclusion zones.
- **Risers and tie-ins.** The connection up to a platform, with expansion spools at the base to absorb pipeline end movement.
- **Flow assurance.** Cold seawater promotes hydrates and wax, so insulation and chemical injection are designed with the pipeline.

## Corrosion protection

- **Coating:** three-layer polyolefin or FBE under concrete, with field joints infilled on the lay vessel.
- **Cathodic protection** by **bracelet anodes** of aluminium-zinc-indium alloy, clamped round the pipe at intervals (typically every 5 to 12 joints) and sized to last the design life without maintenance.
- **Seawater** is a uniform, low-resistivity electrolyte, so sacrificial anodes work well, unlike in most soils.
- Internal corrosion is controlled as onshore.

## Shore approach and landfall

The transition from sea to land is the most complex part of many offshore pipelines:

- breaking waves and strong currents in shallow water give the highest stability loads,
- the seabed moves seasonally,
- the coast is often environmentally sensitive and in use by others.

Methods include a trenched and backfilled approach with the pipe pulled ashore, HDD or microtunnel under the beach, and in very shallow water, installation from land or barges. Saudi Aramco calls these **near-shore** pipelines, and they are covered by the same construction standard as on-land lines.

<Worked title="Stability of a concrete-coated NPS 24 line">

**Given:** OD 610 mm, wall 15.9 mm, 3 mm coating, 50 mm concrete at 3040 kg/m³, empty, seawater 1025 kg/m³, near-bed velocity 1.0 m/s, sand (μ = 0.6).

**Outside diameter:** 0.610 + 0.006 + 0.100 = 0.716 m.

**Weight in air:** steel 233 kg/m, coating 5 kg/m, concrete 318 kg/m. Total 556 kg/m = 5.46 kN/m.

**Buoyancy:** π/4 × 0.716² × 1025 × 9.81 = 4.05 kN/m.

**Submerged weight:** 5.46 − 4.05 = **1.41 kN/m**.

**Hydrodynamic forces:** drag = ½ × 1025 × 0.7 × 0.716 × 1.0² = 257 N/m. Lift = ½ × 1025 × 0.9 × 0.716 × 1.0² = 330 N/m.

**Required weight:** 1.1 × (257 / 0.6 + 330) = **0.83 kN/m**. The pipe is stable.

**At 2.0 m/s** the forces quadruple and the requirement becomes 3.3 kN/m: more than twice the available weight, so more concrete or trenching is needed.

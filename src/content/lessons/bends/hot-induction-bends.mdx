## Why induction bends

Cold field bends need about 40 pipe diameters of radius. At a scraper trap, a valve station, a road crossing or a congested corridor, there is not that much room. A hot induction bend makes the same turn at a radius of 3 to 10 diameters.

Induction bends are factory products, ordered like fittings with a defined radius, angle and tangent lengths. They are the most expensive way to turn a pipeline, so they are used only where needed.

## How they are made

1. A length of **mother pipe** is clamped at its front end to a pivoting radius arm. The arm length sets the bend radius.
2. An **induction coil** around the pipe heats a narrow band, only about 25 to 50 mm wide, to between 850 and 1050 °C.
3. A hydraulic ram pushes the pipe slowly forward through the coil. Because the front end is held by the arm, the hot band is forced to follow a circular arc. Only the hot band deforms.
4. Just behind the coil, a **water or air quench** cools the pipe so the cold material on both sides supports the hot band.
5. The pipe keeps advancing until the required angle is reached. Straight **tangents** are left at each end.
6. The finished bend is usually **heat treated** (quenched and tempered, or tempered) to restore strength and toughness.

Heating and quenching change the steel's microstructure. The properties of the finished bend are those created by the bending and heat treatment, not those of the mother pipe as delivered. This is why bends are qualified by testing.

## What happens to the wall

The outside of the bend (the **extrados**) is stretched and gets thinner. The inside (the **intrados**) is compressed and gets thicker. For a bend of radius $n$ pipe diameters, good estimates are:

$$
\text{extrados thinning} \approx \frac{1}{2n + 1} \qquad\qquad \text{intrados thickening} \approx \frac{1}{2n - 1}
$$

| Radius | Extrados thinning | Intrados thickening |
|---|---|---|
| 3 D | 14 % | 20 % |
| 5 D | 9 % | 11 % |
| 7 D | 7 % | 8 % |
| 10 D | 5 % | 5 % |

## Stress in a bend

Pressure in a curved pipe does not produce uniform hoop stress. The intrados carries **more** than a straight pipe and the extrados **less**:

$$
\frac{\sigma_{intrados}}{\sigma_{straight}} = \frac{4n - 1}{4n - 2} \qquad\qquad \frac{\sigma_{extrados}}{\sigma_{straight}} = \frac{4n + 1}{4n + 2}
$$

Conveniently, the wall gets thicker just where the stress is higher and thinner where it is lower. But the thinning on the extrados is larger than the stress relief there, so the extrados usually governs. The mother pipe must start thicker than the line pipe.

## Choosing the radius

- **5 D** is the common default for mainline bends. All cleaning and inspection pigs pass.
- **3 D** saves space and is accepted by most modern inspection tools. Confirm with the inspection vendor before committing.
- **7 D or 10 D** are used for thin-wall, large-diameter or high-grade pipe, where tighter bends would thin or ovalise too much.
- **1.5 D** (the standard long-radius elbow) is for station piping that is not pigged.

## Specifying a bend

A bend data sheet or purchase specification covers:

- **Geometry:** diameter, radius, angle, tangent lengths at each end, end bevel.
- **Mother pipe:** grade, wall thickness (heavier than the line), process (seamless or LSAW; the seam goes on the neutral axis) and chemistry suited to the heat treatment.
- **Finished properties:** minimum yield and tensile strength, Charpy toughness, maximum hardness (especially for sour service).
- **Dimensional limits:** minimum wall after bending, ovality in the bend and at the ends, no wrinkles.
- **Qualification:** a manufacturing procedure specification and a **test bend**, cut up and tested at the extrados, intrados, neutral axis, weld and transition zones.
- **Inspection:** magnetic particle or ultrasonic testing of the bend surface, wall thickness survey, gauging.
- **Coating:** bends are coated after bending, usually with liquid epoxy or FBE compatible with the line coating.

<Callout type="warn">
The start and stop of bending are **transition zones**, where the heating conditions were not steady. Properties there can differ from the bend body, so qualification tests include them. This is also why long enough tangents matter.
</Callout>

**Given:** line pipe 610 mm × 12.7 mm X65. Required: a 45°, 5 D bend with at least the pressure capacity of the line pipe.

**Extrados:** thinning = 1 / (2 × 5 + 1) = 9.1 %. Stress factor = (4 × 5 + 1) / (4 × 5 + 2) = 0.955.

Wall needed after bending: 12.7 × 0.955 = 12.1 mm. Mother pipe wall: 12.1 / (1 − 0.091) = **13.3 mm**.

**Intrados:** thickening 11.1 %, stress factor (20 − 1) / (20 − 2) = 1.056.

Wall needed after bending: 12.7 × 1.056 = 13.4 mm. Mother pipe wall: 13.4 / 1.111 = 12.1 mm.

**Governing:** the extrados, 13.3 mm. The next standard wall is **14.3 mm**.

**Geometry:** radius 5 × 0.61 = 3.05 m. Arc length = 3.05 × 45° × π / 180 = 2.40 m, plus tangents.

Compare with a cold bend: a 45° turn at 40 D would need three joints and about 36 m.

## Common mistakes

- Ordering mother pipe at line-pipe wall thickness, leaving the extrados under thickness.
- Assuming the bend has the mother pipe's properties. Heat changes them.
- Specifying 1.5 D or 3 D bends without checking the inspection pig's limits.
- Tangents too short for field welding or for hydraulic clamps.
- Forgetting that bend ends must match the line pipe bore, which may need a taper.

## Steel wants to be rust

Iron is found in nature as oxide ore. Making steel puts energy into it, and corrosion is that energy being released as the metal returns to oxide. It cannot be stopped, only slowed to a rate that does not matter over the design life.

## The corrosion cell

Corrosion of buried or wetted steel needs four things at once:

1. **An anode**, where metal dissolves: iron atoms leave the surface as ions, releasing electrons.
2. **A cathode**, where those electrons are consumed, usually by reducing oxygen or hydrogen ions.
3. **A metallic path** carrying electrons from anode to cathode. On a pipeline this is the pipe wall itself.
4. **An electrolyte** carrying ions between them: moist soil, groundwater, or water inside the pipe.

Remove any one of the four and corrosion stops. Every control method does exactly that:

- **Coating** separates the steel from the electrolyte.
- **Cathodic protection** forces the whole pipe surface to be a cathode.
- **Isolation joints** break the metallic path.
- **Dehydration** removes the electrolyte from inside the pipe.

## Why anodes and cathodes form

Different points on the same pipe can have slightly different electrical potentials, and that difference drives current. Causes include:

- **Different metals** connected together (a galvanic couple).
- **New pipe joined to old pipe:** the bright new steel is anodic to the old.
- **Different soils:** steel in clay is anodic to steel in sand.
- **Different oxygen levels:** the bottom of the pipe on undisturbed soil is anodic to the top in aerated backfill.
- **Mill scale, scratches and welds** on the same surface.

Try this: pair clean carbon steel with copper (as in a grounding system) and note which corrodes. Then pair steel with magnesium or zinc to see the principle of a sacrificial anode. Finally try clean steel against rusted steel, the "new pipe next to old pipe" case.

<Callout type="warn">
A small anode connected to a large cathode is the worst combination. All the corrosion current concentrates on the small area. That is why a tiny coating defect on a well-coated pipe connected to bare copper grounding or to reinforced concrete can perforate quickly.
</Callout>

## How fast

Corrosion rate is expressed in millimetres per year. In soil, unprotected steel typically loses 0.02 to 0.2 mm/yr on average, but pits can grow several times faster, and stray current or MIC can exceed 1 mm/yr. Internal rates in wet CO₂ service can reach several mm/yr without control (module M1).

The driving factors in soil are **resistivity** (low is corrosive), moisture, chloride and sulphate content, pH and bacteria. The soil resistivity survey (module M3) is the first indicator:

| Soil resistivity | Corrosivity |
|---|---|
| Below 10 ohm·m | Severe (sabkha, saline clay) |
| 10 to 50 ohm·m | Corrosive |
| 50 to 100 ohm·m | Moderate |
| Above 100 ohm·m | Mild (dry sand, rock) |

## The control strategy

Pipelines are protected by **two independent barriers** used together:

- **Coating** is the primary barrier. It isolates more than 99 % of the surface.
- **Cathodic protection** protects the steel at the small percentage of defects every coating has.

Each covers the other's weakness. Coating alone always has holidays. CP alone on bare pipe would need enormous current. Together they are cheap and effective. Internal corrosion is controlled separately (later lesson).

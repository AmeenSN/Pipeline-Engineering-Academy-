## Borrowed current

A coated steel pipeline is a long, well-insulated conductor lying in the ground. It picks up electrical energy from neighbouring systems easily. Where that current **enters** the pipe, the steel is protected. Where it **leaves** to return to its source, metal is lost.

## DC stray current

Sources of direct current in the ground:

- **Another pipeline's cathodic protection.** Current from a foreign ground bed is picked up by your pipe where it passes near the bed, travels along it, and discharges where your pipe crosses or approaches the foreign pipeline.
- **DC traction:** metro, tram and some railway systems return current through the rails, and part of it leaks into the ground.
- **HVDC transmission electrodes** during monopolar operation.
- **Mining and welding** equipment.

One ampere of DC leaving steel removes about 9 kg of metal a year. Concentrated at a single coating holiday, that perforates the wall in months.

**Detecting it:** potential surveys with the foreign system switched on and off, data loggers at test posts to catch fluctuating potentials from traction systems, and current measurements along the pipe.

**Mitigation:**

- **Resistive bond** between the two structures at the discharge point, so current returns through a wire instead of through the soil.
- **Drainage bond with a diode** back to a traction system's negative return.
- **Additional local anodes** at the discharge area.
- **Coating in top condition** at crossings, and physical separation from foreign ground beds.
- **Coordination:** operators notify each other of new CP installations and test jointly.

## AC interference

High-voltage overhead power lines are frequently routed in the same corridors as pipelines. Three mechanisms transfer AC energy to the pipe:

- **Inductive coupling.** The alternating magnetic field around the power conductors induces a voltage along a parallel pipeline, like a transformer. It acts continuously in normal operation and grows with parallel length, load current and how well the pipe is coated.
- **Conductive (resistive) coupling.** During a ground fault or lightning strike, large current enters the earth at a tower and raises the soil potential around it. The voltage across the coating can puncture it or arc to the pipe.
- **Capacitive coupling.** Affects pipe above ground and insulated from earth, such as a welded string on skids during construction.

Modern coatings make this worse, not better. A pipe with excellent coating has almost no natural path to earth, so induced voltage builds up along it.

Two separate concerns follow:

**Safety.** Touch voltage at valves, test posts and exposed pipe. Limits of about 15 V in steady state are widely used, with much higher short-duration limits under fault conditions.

**AC corrosion.** AC discharging at small coating holidays can corrode steel even when cathodic protection criteria are met. The risk indicator is the AC current density at a small holiday:

$$
J_{ac} = \frac{8 \, V_{ac}}{\rho \, \pi \, d}
$$

with $V_{ac}$ the pipe-to-earth AC voltage, $\rho$ the soil resistivity and $d$ the holiday diameter (0.0113 m for the standard 1 cm² holiday). Low-resistivity soil is the dangerous case.

## AC mitigation

- **Grounding** the pipeline at intervals to bring the voltage down: zinc ribbon laid in the trench beside the pipe, or copper grounding conductors.
- **DC decouplers** between pipe and grounding. These solid-state devices pass AC freely but block DC, so the grounding does not drain the cathodic protection.
- **Gradient control mats** buried around valves and test posts, bonded to the pipe, so that a person standing there is at the same potential as what they touch.
- **Dead-front test posts** and insulated enclosures.
- **Separation** from tower footings and substation grounds, and crossing power lines at right angles.
- **Construction precautions:** temporary grounding of the welded string, bonding across joints before cutting, and safe working distances for cranes under conductors.
- **Monitoring:** AC voltage logging and AC coupons that measure actual current density.

<Callout type="warn">
Induced AC on a pipe string lying on skids parallel to a power line can deliver a dangerous shock. Ground the string, and bond across any gap before a worker bridges it with their hands.
</Callout>

An **AC interference study** is carried out at design stage wherever a pipeline runs parallel to a high-voltage line for more than a few hundred metres, modelling steady-state and fault conditions and sizing the mitigation.

<Worked title="AC corrosion risk in sabkha">

**Given:** 8 V AC measured on the pipeline, soil resistivity 5 ohm·m.

$$
J_{ac} = \frac{8 \times 8}{5 \times \pi \times 0.0113} = 361 \text{ A/m}^2
$$

Far above 100 A/m²: AC corrosion is expected at small holidays, even though 8 V is below the 15 V touch limit.

**In dry sand at 200 ohm·m** the same 8 V gives 9 A/m²: low risk.

**Mitigation target:** to get below 30 A/m² in the sabkha, the voltage must come down to about 0.7 V, which needs closely spaced grounding through decouplers.

</Worked>

## Diameter is an economic choice

For a given flow, a larger pipe costs more to buy and build but loses less pressure to friction, so it needs fewer or smaller pump or compressor stations. The hydraulic design finds the diameter and operating pressure that give the lowest total cost over the life of the line.

## Friction loss

The pressure lost to friction along a pipe is given by the Darcy-Weisbach equation:

$$
\Delta P = f \, \frac{L}{D} \, \frac{\rho \, v^2}{2}
$$

- $f$ is the friction factor, which depends on Reynolds number and pipe roughness.
- $L$ and $D$ are the length and inside diameter.
- $\rho$ is density and $v$ is velocity.

Two results follow. Pressure drop rises with the **square** of flow rate. And for the same flow, it falls roughly with the **fifth power** of diameter: going from NPS 20 to NPS 24 cuts friction by about 60 %.

The Reynolds number, $Re = \rho v D / \mu$, tells you whether flow is laminar (below about 2300) or turbulent. Pipelines are almost always turbulent.

## Elevation

Liquid pipelines also gain or lose pressure with height:

$$
\Delta P_{static} = \rho \, g \, \Delta z
$$

For crude oil this is about 0.83 bar for every 10 m. A pipeline climbing 500 m needs over 40 bar just to lift the oil. Going downhill, the same effect can raise pressure above MAOP in a valley even when the pump is off: the **static** or shut-in condition must always be checked.

For gas, density is low, so elevation matters much less, but gas is compressible, so its velocity rises as pressure falls along the line.

## MAOP

The **maximum allowable operating pressure** is the highest pressure at which the pipeline may be operated. It is the lowest of:

- the design pressure of the pipe (from wall thickness),
- the hydrotest pressure divided by the test factor,
- the rating of the weakest component (flanges, valves).

Operating pressure, including normal variations, must stay at or below MAOP at every point. Because of elevation, the governing point is often a low spot, not the pump discharge.

## Surge

When flow is stopped suddenly, by a valve closing or a pump tripping, the moving liquid column piles up against the closure. Its kinetic energy becomes pressure. This is surge, or water hammer. The Joukowsky equation gives the rise for an instant stop:

$$
\Delta P = \rho \, a \, \Delta v
$$

where $a$ is the speed of the pressure wave in the pipe, around 1000 to 1200 m/s for oil or water in steel. Every 1 m/s of velocity stopped produces roughly 9 to 12 bar.

The full rise occurs if the valve closes faster than the time the wave takes to travel to the far end and back, $2L / a$. On a long pipeline that can be several minutes, so most ordinary valve closures count as "fast".

Liquid pipeline codes limit surge to **110 % of the design pressure**. If the calculated surge exceeds that, the designer adds:

- slower valve closure times,
- surge relief valves and tanks,
- pump trip logic and controlled shutdown sequences,
- or a higher design pressure near the valve.

<Callout type="warn">
Gas pipelines do not suffer liquid surge, because gas compresses. Liquid lines, including water injection and NGL lines, always need a surge study.
</Callout>

<Worked title="Friction loss in a 100 km crude line">

**Given:** NPS 24 × 12.7 mm (inside diameter 584.6 mm), 2000 m³/h of crude, density 850 kg/m³, viscosity 8 cP, roughness 0.045 mm.

**Velocity:** area = π/4 × 0.5846² = 0.2684 m². v = (2000 / 3600) / 0.2684 = **2.07 m/s**.

**Reynolds number:** 850 × 2.07 × 0.5846 / 0.008 = 128 600. Turbulent.

**Friction factor** (Swamee-Jain): f = 0.0175.

**Pressure gradient:**

$$
\frac{\Delta P}{L} = \frac{0.0175}{0.5846} \times \frac{850 \times 2.07^2}{2} = 54.5 \text{ Pa/m} = 0.545 \text{ bar/km}
$$

**Over 100 km:** about **54 bar** of friction loss. With 80 bar at the pump and 50 m of climb (4 bar), about 22 bar arrives.

**Surge:** wave speed about 1140 m/s, so an instant stop gives 850 × 1140 × 2.07 = 20 bar.

</Worked>

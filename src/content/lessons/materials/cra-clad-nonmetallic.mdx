## The material selection question

Carbon steel is always the first candidate. The question is whether it can last the design life with a sensible corrosion allowance and a corrosion control programme that will really be maintained. If it cannot, a more resistant material is needed.

Internal corrosion in oil and gas pipelines is driven mainly by:

- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** dissolved in water, forming carbonic acid ("sweet" corrosion).
- **Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S)**, which causes cracking as well as corrosion ("sour" service).
- **Chlorides, oxygen and bacteria**, mainly in water injection and produced water lines.

No free water means no corrosion. Dry gas and stabilised crude lines are normally carbon steel.

## Estimating CO₂ corrosion

The classic de Waard and Milliams equation gives the corrosion rate of bare carbon steel in wet CO₂:

$$
\log V_{cor} = 5.8 - \frac{1710}{T} + 0.67 \, \log p_{CO_2}
$$

with $V_{cor}$ in mm per year, $T$ in kelvin and $p_{CO_2}$ (the CO₂ partial pressure) in bar. Partial pressure is total pressure multiplied by the mole fraction of CO₂ in the gas.

This is a deliberately conservative starting point. Real rates are lowered by protective iron carbonate scale, higher pH, oil wetting and glycol, and modern models correct for these.

## Inhibition and availability

Corrosion inhibitor can cut the rate to around 0.1 mm per year, but only while it is being injected. The key number is **availability**: the fraction of time the inhibition system works as intended.

$$
\text{wall loss} = \left[ A \times V_{inhibited} + (1 - A) \times V_{cor} \right] \times \text{design life}
$$

At a bare rate of several millimetres a year, the few percent of time without inhibitor does almost all the damage.

Try this: keep the default hot, high-CO₂ case and move inhibitor availability from 95 % to 99 %. The wall loss falls sharply. Then raise the H₂S partial pressure past 0.3 kPa and note the sour-service flag.

As a rule of thumb, a corrosion allowance of 1.5 to 3 mm is normal, up to 6 mm is heavy but possible, and beyond that carbon steel is the wrong material.

## The sour threshold

ISO 15156 (NACE MR0175) defines sour service for carbon steel as an H₂S partial pressure of **0.3 kPa (0.05 psi)** or more in the presence of water. Above it, materials must be resistant to sulphide stress cracking. For carbon steel that means controlled hardness and qualified chemistry (see the grades lesson). For alloys, each grade has its own limits of H₂S, chloride, temperature and pH.

## The alternatives

### Corrosion-resistant alloys

| Alloy | What it resists | Main limits |
|---|---|---|
| 13Cr martensitic stainless | Wet CO₂, moderate temperature | Little H₂S tolerance, difficult field welding |
| 316L austenitic stainless | CO₂; used as cladding | Chloride pitting and stress cracking when hot |
| 22Cr duplex | CO₂ with some H₂S, chlorides | About 150 °C, strict welding control, external chloride cracking |
| 25Cr super duplex | As duplex, more severe | Cost, heat-treatment sensitivity |
| Alloy 825 / 625 (nickel alloys) | Severe sour, high chloride, hot | Cost: normally used only as cladding |

### Non-metallic pipe

Non-metallic pipe removes corrosion as a failure mode and replaces it with others: temperature, pressure, permeation, impact damage and joint integrity.

- **Reinforced thermoplastic pipe (RTP)** is spooled and laid quickly with few joints. It suits flowlines and water lines within its pressure and temperature rating.
- **Glass-reinforced epoxy (GRE, also called RTR)** is rigid pipe in joints with bonded or threaded connections. It is common for water injection and produced water.
- **HDPE liners** pulled into carbon steel lines protect the steel for water service and rehabilitate corroded lines.

## Comparing on life-cycle cost

The decision is rarely made on purchase price. Compare over the full design life:

- **Capital cost:** pipe, welding (alloy welding is slow), coating, installation.
- **Operating cost:** inhibitor chemicals, injection equipment, monitoring, pigging, inspection runs.
- **Risk cost:** the consequence of a leak and the realism of maintaining 95 % or better inhibitor availability at a remote site for 25 years.

Short, hot, very corrosive lines favour alloy. Long, cooler, mildly corrosive lines favour inhibited carbon steel.

<Worked title="Screening a hot wet gas flowline">

**Given:** 60 °C, total pressure 100 bar with 2 mol % CO₂, so CO₂ partial pressure is 2 bar. Design life 25 years. Negligible H₂S.

**Bare-steel rate:**

$$
\log V_{cor} = 5.8 - \frac{1710}{333} + 0.67 \log(2) = 0.869 \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_{cor} = 7.4 \text{ mm/yr}
$$

**With inhibitor at 95 % availability** (inhibited rate 0.1 mm/yr):

$$
0.95 \times 0.1 + 0.05 \times 7.4 = 0.465 \text{ mm/yr} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 11.6 \text{ mm in 25 years}
$$

That is far beyond a practical allowance.

**At 99 % availability:** 0.99 × 0.1 + 0.01 × 7.4 = 0.173 mm/yr, or 4.3 mm in 25 years. Feasible with a heavy allowance, but only if 99 % availability is truly achievable.

**Conclusion:** unless a refined corrosion model reduces the bare rate substantially, this line is a candidate for CRA-clad or solid 13Cr pipe.

</Worked>

## Common mistakes

- Using total pressure instead of CO₂ partial pressure.
- Assuming 100 % inhibitor availability.
- Selecting duplex stainless steel without checking its temperature and H₂S limits.
- Forgetting that non-metallic lines still need metallic connectors, which can corrode.
- Ignoring external corrosion: alloy pipe buried in sabkha may still need coating.

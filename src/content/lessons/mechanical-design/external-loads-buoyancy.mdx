## Loads from above

A buried pipe is a flexible ring. It carries vertical load partly through its own stiffness and partly by pushing sideways against the backfill. Two loads act on the crown.

**Soil load.** For a flexible steel pipe the usual estimate is the weight of the soil prism directly above it:

$$
P_s = \gamma \, H
$$

**Live load.** A wheel on the surface spreads its load through the soil. Directly below a point load $P_w$, Boussinesq gives:

$$
P_l = \frac{3 \, P_w}{2 \pi H^2} \times \text{impact factor}
$$

Soil load rises with depth. Traffic load falls with the **square** of depth. Their sum has a minimum, usually between 1 and 2 m of cover.

## Ovalisation

Vertical pressure squashes the ring into an oval. The modified Iowa formula estimates the change in diameter:

$$
\frac{\Delta y}{D} = \frac{D_l \, K \, P}{\dfrac{E I}{R^3} + 0.061 \, E'}
$$

- $E I / R^3$ is the ring stiffness of the pipe wall ($I = t^3 / 12$ per unit length).
- $E'$ is the modulus of soil reaction: how firmly the backfill supports the sides of the pipe.
- $K$ is a bedding constant (about 0.1) and $D_l$ a time-lag factor (1.0 to 1.5).

Typical limits are 3 % for coated steel line pipe, and less where intelligent pigs must pass. For thin-wall pipe the soil term dominates the denominator, which is why **backfill compaction matters more than wall thickness** for ovality.

<Callout type="note">
Internal pressure re-rounds the pipe and helps resist ovalisation. The worst case for external load is therefore usually the **depressurised** line, for example during construction or shutdown.

## Road and railroad crossings

The explorer gives a quick screening estimate. A formal crossing design uses **API RP 1102**, which provides design curves for the circumferential and longitudinal stresses caused by earth load and by cyclic wheel load, then checks:

- total effective stress against the allowable, and
- **fatigue** of the girth weld and the longitudinal seam under repeated axle passes.

Crossings are covered in depth in module M6.

<Callout type="aramco">
SAES-L-460 governs pipeline crossings under roads and railroads. Aramco practice generally favours thicker-wall uncased carrier pipe over casings, because a casing shields the pipe from cathodic protection and can short to the carrier. Minimum cover at crossings is greater than in open desert.
</Callout>

## Minimum cover

| Location | ASME B31.4 (liquid) | ASME B31.8 (gas) |
|---|---|---|
| Open country, normal excavation | 0.75 m (30 in) | 0.61 m (24 in) in Class 1 |
| Industrial, commercial, residential | 0.9 m (36 in) | 0.76 m (30 in) in Class 2 to 4 |
| Road and rail drainage ditches | 0.9 m (36 in) | 0.9 m (36 in) |
| River and stream crossings | 1.2 m (48 in) | By engineering assessment |

Company standards are usually stricter. Projects in Saudi Arabia commonly specify about 1 m in open desert and more at crossings, in wadis and in moving dune areas.

## Buoyancy

Below the water table, a pipe is pushed up by the weight of the fluid it displaces. Large-diameter pipe is mostly air: an empty NPS 30 line displaces about 470 kg of water per metre but weighs only about 180 kg per metre. It will float.

$$
SF = \frac{W_{pipe} + W_{coating} + W_{contents}}{\rho_f \, g \, \dfrac{\pi D_o^2}{4}} \geq 1.1
$$

Check the **empty** pipe, because that is its state during construction and after any shutdown. Remember that the surrounding fluid may be heavier than water:

- Sabkha brine can reach 1150 to 1200 kg/m³.
- Liquefied backfill behaves like a fluid of 1500 kg/m³ or more.

### Buoyancy control methods

- **Continuous concrete weight coating**, applied at the coating yard. Standard for sabkha, marsh and offshore.
- **Set-on or bolt-on concrete weights** at intervals. Useful for short wet sections.
- **Screw anchors** with straps. Efficient where the ground below is firm.
- **Geotextile bag weights** filled with gravel. Gentle on coating.
- **Deeper burial** with engineered backfill, where the backfill weight can be relied on.

<Worked title="Concrete coating for an NPS 30 line in sabkha">

**Given:** OD 762 mm, wall 9.5 mm, 3 mm polyethylene coating, empty, sabkha brine at 1150 kg/m³, concrete density 3040 kg/m³, target safety factor 1.1.

**Weight without concrete:** steel 176.3 kg/m plus coating 6.9 kg/m gives 183.2 kg/m, or 1.80 kN/m.

**Buoyancy without concrete:** displaced area π/4 × 0.768² = 0.463 m².

$$
B = 0.463 \times 1150 \times 9.81 = 5.23 \text{ kN/m} \qquad SF = \frac{1.80}{5.23} = 0.34
$$

The bare pipe floats easily.

**With concrete:** each millimetre of concrete adds weight but also adds displaced volume. Solving for the thickness where weight equals 1.1 × buoyancy gives an outer diameter of 0.937 m, so the concrete thickness is **85 mm**.

</Worked>

## Common mistakes

- Assuming more cover is always safer. Deep burial raises soil load and makes future repair harder.
- Using the density of fresh water in sabkha or tidal areas.
- Checking buoyancy with the pipe full of product.
- Relying on backfill weight in a trench that can flood before backfilling.

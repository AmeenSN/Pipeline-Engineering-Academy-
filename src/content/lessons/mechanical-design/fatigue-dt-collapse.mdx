## Fatigue: failure below yield

A pipeline that passes every static check can still crack if its stress keeps changing. Each cycle opens tiny flaws at weld toes a little further. After enough cycles a crack grows through the wall.

Sources of cyclic stress in pipelines:

- **Pressure cycling.** Batched liquid lines, pump starts and stops, and gas lines with daily demand swings (line packing).
- **Thermal cycling** from shutdowns and restarts.
- **Traffic** at road and rail crossings.
- **Vibration** from spans (previous lesson) or from equipment.

Liquid lines generally see far more severe pressure cycling than gas lines, because liquid is nearly incompressible and pressure follows every operational change.

## S-N curves

Fatigue strength is described by an S-N curve: the number of cycles to failure $N$ at a constant stress **range** $\Delta\sigma$.

$$
\log N = \log \bar{a} - m \, \log \Delta\sigma
$$

With $m = 3$ for welded steel, the relationship is steep: **doubling the stress range cuts the life by a factor of eight**. Different weld details have different curves. A ground-flush weld performs better than one with a root backing strip or misalignment.

For pressure cycling the stress range is simply the hoop stress range:

$$
\Delta\sigma = \frac{\Delta P \, D}{2 \, t}
$$

## Miner's rule

Real pipelines see a mix of cycle sizes. Miner's rule adds up the damage from each:

$$
D_{fat} = \sum \frac{n_i}{N_i} \leq \frac{1}{DFF}
$$

$n_i$ is the number of cycles at range $i$, $N_i$ is the life at that range, and DFF is the design fatigue factor (3 to 10, depending on consequence and on whether the weld can be inspected).

Fatigue cracks start at stress concentrations. Dents, gouges, seam-weld peaking and girth-weld misalignment all multiply the local stress range. A dent that looks harmless in a static check can be the life-limiting feature on a heavily cycled line.

## What D/t controls

The diameter-to-thickness ratio is a quick indicator of how the pipe will behave beyond pressure containment.

| D/t | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Below about 20 | Thick wall. Hard to cold bend; thin-wall formulas start to lose accuracy. |
| 20 to 60 | Normal range for transmission lines. |
| 60 to 96 | Thin. Needs care in handling, stacking and bending; ovality control matters. |
| Above about 96 | Very thin. Prone to denting, buckling in field bends and collapse; avoided by most company standards. |

High D/t also limits cold field bending (wrinkles form on the inside of the bend) and lowers resistance to buckling under bending and external pressure.

## External pressure collapse

A pipe under external pressure fails by flattening. Two idealised limits bound the behaviour:

$$
p_{el} = \frac{2 E}{1 - \nu^2} \left( \frac{t}{D} \right)^3 \qquad\qquad p_p = 2 \, \sigma_y \, \frac{t}{D}
$$

Thin pipe collapses **elastically**: the limit depends on stiffness and falls with the cube of D/t. Thick pipe collapses **plastically**: the limit depends on yield strength. Real pipe lies below both, and initial out-of-roundness lowers it further. DNV-ST-F101 combines them as:

$$
(p_c - p_{el})(p_c^2 - p_p^2) = p_c \, p_{el} \, p_p \, f_0 \, \frac{D}{t}
$$

where $f_0$ is the initial ovality.

Collapse governs offshore installation, when the pipe is empty at full water depth. Onshore it matters for:

- deep river and lake crossings,
- HDD installations, where drilling mud pressure acts on an empty pipe,
- vacuum conditions after liquid column separation, and
- casings and large low-pressure lines under deep fill.

<Worked title="Fatigue life of a batched NPS 20 liquid line">

**Given:** OD 508 mm, wall 9.5 mm, pressure swings of 6 MPa (60 bar), 4 times per day. Girth welds with some misalignment: curve E ($\log \bar{a}$ = 12.010, m = 3). Design fatigue factor 5.

**Stress range:**

$$
\Delta\sigma = \frac{6 \times 508}{2 \times 9.5} = 160.4 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Cycles to failure:**

$$
\log N = 12.010 - 3 \log(160.4) = 5.394 \quad\Rightarrow\quad N = 248\,000 \text{ cycles}
$$

**Life:** 4 × 365 = 1460 cycles per year, so 248 000 / 1460 = 170 years calculated life.

**Design life:** 170 / 5 = **34 years**.

For a 40-year service life this is not enough. Options are a 10.3 mm wall (stress range 148 MPa, design life 43 years), tighter weld alignment to qualify for curve D, or smaller pressure swings.

</Worked>

<Worked title="Maximum water depth for an empty NPS 24 pipe">

**Given:** OD 610 mm, wall 15.9 mm (D/t = 38.4), X65, initial ovality 1 %.

**Elastic limit:** $p_{el}$ = 2 × 207 000 / 0.91 × (15.9 / 610)³ = 8.06 MPa.

**Plastic limit:** $p_p$ = 2 × 450 × 15.9 / 610 = 23.5 MPa.

**Collapse pressure** from the combined equation: $p_c$ = 7.04 MPa. The elastic limit clearly governs at this D/t.

**Allowable depth** with a resistance factor of 1.3: 7.04 / 1.3 = 5.41 MPa, which is the pressure of about **540 m** of seawater.

</Worked>

## Common mistakes

- Using maximum pressure instead of pressure **range** for fatigue.
- Ignoring fatigue on liquid lines because "the stress is below yield".
- Applying a parent-metal S-N curve to a girth weld.
- Forgetting the empty-pipe collapse case for HDD crossings.

## Two stresses at every point

Take a small square of pipe wall. Pressure stretches it around the circumference (**hoop stress**, $\sigma_h$). At the same time the pipe is pulled or pushed along its axis (**longitudinal stress**, $\sigma_L$). Hoop stress depends only on pressure and geometry. Longitudinal stress depends on whether the pipe is free to move.

## Restrained or unrestrained?

**Unrestrained pipe** can change length. Examples are above-ground pipe with expansion loops, and the last stretch of a buried line as it comes out of the ground. Pressure on closed ends and elbows pulls the pipe axially:

$$
\sigma_L = \frac{P D}{4 t} = \frac{\sigma_h}{2}
$$

**Restrained pipe** cannot change length. A long buried pipeline is held by soil friction, so two things happen:

- **Poisson effect.** Hoop tension makes the pipe want to shorten. The soil stops it, which leaves an axial tension of $\nu \sigma_h$ (about 30 % of the hoop stress).
- **Thermal effect.** A pipe that warms by $\Delta T$ wants to grow by $\alpha \Delta T$ per unit length. The soil stops that too, which creates compression of $E \alpha \Delta T$.

$$
\sigma_L = \nu \, \sigma_h - E \, \alpha \, (T_2 - T_1)
$$

For steel, $E \alpha \approx 2.4$ MPa per °C. A line tied in at 25 °C and operated at 75 °C picks up about 121 MPa of compression from temperature alone.

<Callout type="note">
$T_1$ is the temperature at which the pipe was **tied in** (locked into the ground), and $T_2$ is the operating temperature. In hot climates, tying in during the heat of the day raises $T_1$ and directly lowers compressive stress. This is why construction specifications control tie-in temperature.
</Callout>

## Combining the stresses

Steel yields under a combination of stresses, not under each one separately. Hoop tension together with axial **compression** is the worst pairing, because the two stresses work together to shear the material.

The codes allow either criterion:

$$
\sigma_{eq} = \sqrt{\sigma_h^2 - \sigma_h \sigma_L + \sigma_L^2} \quad \text{(Von Mises)}
$$

$$
\sigma_{eq} = \left| \sigma_h - \sigma_L \right| \quad \text{(Tresca, when the signs differ)}
$$

and limit the result to 0.90 × SMYS for restrained pipe.

## Reading the yield envelope

The ellipse in the right-hand plot is the Von Mises yield surface. Three things are worth noticing:

- In the upper-right quadrant (both stresses tensile), the envelope bulges outward. Axial tension up to half the hoop stress actually helps.
- In the upper-left quadrant (hoop tension, axial compression), the envelope cuts in sharply. This is where hot buried pipelines operate.
- With zero pressure and a hot line, the point sits on the left of the horizontal axis: pure compression. This is the condition that drives upheaval buckling.

## Other stresses to add

The explorer covers pressure and temperature. A full analysis also adds:

- **Bending stress** from elastic bends, settlement and spans: $\sigma_b = E D / (2 R)$ for an elastic bend of radius R.
- **External loads** from soil and traffic (later lesson).
- **Occasional loads** such as surge, earthquake and hydrotest.

<Worked title="Combined stress in a buried NPS 24 crude line">

**Given:** OD 610 mm, wall 12.7 mm, X65 (450 MPa), P = 9.6 MPa, tie-in at 30 °C, operating at 75 °C (ΔT = 45 °C), E = 207 000 MPa, α = 11.7 × 10⁻⁶ /°C, ν = 0.3.

**Hoop stress**

$$
\sigma_h = \frac{9.6 \times 610}{2 \times 12.7} = 230.6 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Longitudinal stress**

$$
\sigma_L = 0.3 \times 230.6 - 207000 \times 11.7 \times 10^{-6} \times 45 = 69.2 - 109.0 = -39.8 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Von Mises equivalent**

$$
\sigma_{eq} = \sqrt{230.6^2 - (230.6)(-39.8) + (-39.8)^2} = 252.9 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Check:** 0.90 × 450 = 405 MPa. Utilisation is 62 %, so the line passes with margin.

</Worked>

## Common mistakes

- Using the unrestrained formula ($\sigma_h / 2$) for a buried line.
- Getting the sign of the thermal term wrong. Heating a restrained pipe causes compression.
- Using ambient temperature instead of the actual tie-in temperature for $T_1$.
- Forgetting the shutdown case: a cold, depressurised line in winter can go into axial tension.

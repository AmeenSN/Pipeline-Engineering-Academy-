## Why divide the line

A pipeline hundreds of kilometres long holds an enormous inventory. Sectionalizing valves (also called block valves or mainline valves) let the operator:

- **limit the release** if the line leaks or ruptures, by closing the valves either side,
- **isolate a section** for repair, modification or a tie-in without emptying the whole line,
- **blow down or drain** one section at a time,
- separate sections for **commissioning and testing**.

Valves do not prevent a failure, and they do not affect the first minutes of a gas rupture, which are driven by the gas already in the pipe near the break. They reduce how long the release lasts and how much is lost.

## Spacing for gas pipelines

ASME B31.8 sets a maximum spacing by location class, so that every point on the line is within a set distance of a valve:

| Location class | Maximum spacing | Farthest point from a valve |
|---|---|---|
| Class 1 | 32 km (20 miles) | 16 km |
| Class 2 | 24 km (15 miles) | 12 km |
| Class 3 | 16 km (10 miles) | 8 km |
| Class 4 | 8 km (5 miles) | 4 km |

Closer spacing in populated areas limits both the duration of a fire and the time to make the line safe. The code allows spacing to be adjusted slightly so that valves fall at accessible locations.

## Placement for liquid pipelines

ASME B31.4 has no general spacing table. Valves are located by engineering assessment, because what matters for a liquid release is the **terrain**: once the valves are closed, liquid drains by gravity to the break from the high points on each side.

- **Both sides of major rivers** and water bodies, to protect water supplies.
- **Either side of populated or environmentally sensitive areas.**
- At locations that minimise **drain-down volume**: at the tops of slopes and before long descents.
- **Check valves** on uphill-flowing sections, which close automatically and stop the column running back.
- At pump stations and at intervals that make maintenance practical.

A **spill volume analysis** of the route profile is used to optimise valve positions.

## How valves are operated

| Type | How it closes | Typical response | Where used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manual | A person travels to site and operates a gearbox | Hours | Remote Class 1 areas, low-consequence lines |
| Remotely operated (ROV) | Control room command through SCADA to an actuator | Minutes | Most modern transmission lines |
| Automatic (ASV / line-break) | Local sensing of low pressure or a high rate of pressure drop | Minutes, with no communication needed | Sites with unreliable communications, critical crossings |
| Emergency shutdown (ESD) | Station safety system | Seconds to minutes | Station inlets and outlets |

**Actuators** on mainline valves are gas-over-oil (using the pipeline's own gas pressure), electro-hydraulic, or electric. They need a stored energy supply so the valve can close on loss of power.

Automatic line-break valves must be set carefully. A rate-of-pressure-drop setting that is too sensitive closes the valve during normal transients, such as a compressor trip, which is disruptive and can itself be hazardous.

## The valve station

A mainline valve site typically contains:

- the **mainline valve**: full-bore ball or through-conduit gate, welded ends, often buried with a stem extension,
- a **bypass** around it with smaller valves, for equalising pressure before opening and for controlled filling,
- **blowdown connections** each side, with a vent stack on gas lines sized to depressurise the section in a set time,
- **pressure and temperature transmitters** each side,
- the **actuator, power supply** (solar and batteries at remote sites) and **remote terminal unit**,
- **cathodic protection** test facilities and often a rectifier,
- **fencing, lighting and access road**, with the site located away from roads and housing where possible.

Valves are also placed so that a pig can pass: the piggable route is always through the full-bore mainline valve.

## Opening a closed valve

A mainline valve with full pipeline pressure on one side and none on the other must not simply be opened. The pressure is first **equalised through the bypass**. Opening against a large differential damages the seats and, on a liquid line, sends a surge down the empty side.

## One drawing, everything in it

A cross-country pipeline may be hundreds of kilometres long. It is drawn as a series of alignment sheets, each covering a kilometre or more. Every sheet has the same layout, so that anyone can find the same information in the same place.

An alignment sheet has three parts, stacked vertically and sharing one horizontal scale:

- **Plan** at the top: a map view of the route and its surroundings.
- **Profile** in the middle: a side view of the ground and the pipe.
- **Data bands** at the bottom: rows of information, each aligned with the plan and profile above.

Because they share a scale, you can drop a vertical line anywhere and read everything about that point.

## The plan

The plan shows the pipeline centreline on a strip of topography or imagery, with:

- **Points of intersection (PI)** where the route changes direction, each with coordinates and deflection angle.
- Features crossed or nearby: roads, tracks, wadis, power lines, fences, buildings, existing pipelines.
- **Right-of-way limits** and any temporary working areas.
- North arrow, grid and match lines to the neighbouring sheets.

## The profile

The profile plots elevation against distance, usually with the vertical scale exaggerated five or ten times so that slopes are visible. It shows:

- the **existing ground line** from the topographic survey,
- the **pipe line** (top of pipe or bottom of trench) set by the designer,
- depth of cover, by the gap between them,
- crossings in section, with the pipe passing under each,
- positions of vertical bends: sag bends and overbends.

<Callout type="warn">
Because the vertical scale is exaggerated, slopes and bends look far steeper on the profile than they are. Always read angles from the bend band or schedule, never by measuring the drawing.
</Callout>

## Stationing

Distance along the pipeline is measured from its origin and written as a **station** or **kilometre point (KP)**, for example KP 12+350 means 12 350 m from the start. Stationing follows the slope distance or the horizontal distance, depending on the project convention: check the drawing notes, because on hilly routes the two differ.

When a re-route changes the length, a **station equation** is used so that stations beyond it do not all have to be renumbered.

## The data bands

Typical bands, though every company has its own set:

| Band | What it records |
|---|---|
| Pipe | Diameter, wall thickness, grade, manufacturing type |
| Location class / design factor | The basis for the wall thickness |
| Coating | External coating, concrete coating, field joint type |
| Cover | Minimum depth of cover required |
| Bends | Type, angle and direction of each bend |
| Crossings | Type, reference drawing, method |
| Soil / terrain | Rock, sand, sabkha: drives trench and padding |
| Cathodic protection | Test posts, anodes, bonding |
| Hydrotest | Test section limits and test pressure |
| Land | Ownership or permit references |
| Material take-off | Quantities for the sheet |

## Design and as-built

The **issued-for-construction** sheet shows what is intended. During construction the surveyor records what was actually built, and the sheet is re-issued **as-built** with:

- surveyed position and level of the pipe as laid, typically at every weld,
- actual cover,
- weld numbers and pipe joint (heat) numbers,
- actual bend positions and angles,
- any re-routes and their approvals.

The as-built sheets and their database are the foundation of the operator's integrity management for the next fifty years.

<Callout type="aramco">

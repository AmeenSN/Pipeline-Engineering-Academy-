## Why water must go

Water left in a pipeline after testing causes:

- **Corrosion,** immediately, especially if the test water was saline or the line stands idle before start-up.
- **Hydrates** in gas pipelines: ice-like solids of water and gas that form at high pressure and can block the line completely.
- **Off-specification product:** sales gas and many liquid products have strict water limits.
- **Ice** in cold service and during depressurisation.

How dry the line must be depends on the product. A crude oil line needs only bulk dewatering. A sales gas, ethylene or LNG feed line needs to be thoroughly dry.

## Dewatering

Bulk water is pushed out by a **pig train** driven by compressed air (or by the product itself in some liquid lines):

- Several pigs run in sequence: bi-directional disc or cup pigs first, then foam pigs to wipe the film left behind.
- Pig speed is kept steady, around 0.5 to 1 m/s. Too fast and the pig rides over the water.
- Water is discharged through a controlled outlet to the approved disposal point.
- In hilly terrain, back-pressure is held at the outlet so the water column does not run away downhill and leave the pigs behind.

After dewatering, a thin film remains on the wall, plus pools at low points, valve bodies and dead legs. For a large pipeline this is still many cubic metres.

## How dry is dry enough

Dryness is specified as a **water dew point**: the temperature at which the air or gas in the line would start to condense water. The lower the dew point, the drier the line.

| Service | Typical target dew point (at atmospheric pressure) |
|---|---|
| Crude oil, water injection | Bulk dewatering only |
| Refined products | Dewatered and swabbed |
| Natural gas transmission | About −20 °C |
| Sour or CO₂-rich gas | −20 °C to −40 °C |
| Ethylene, LPG, chemical feedstock | −40 °C or lower |

After reaching the target, the line is shut in for a **soak test**, typically 12 to 24 hours, and the dew point measured again. If water remains in pools it will evaporate during the soak and the dew point will rise.

## Purging and inerting

Hydrocarbon gas and air must never be allowed to mix inside the pipeline. Before gas is introduced:

- A **slug of nitrogen**, often between pigs, is placed between the air in the line and the incoming gas, or the whole line is purged with nitrogen until oxygen is below a few percent.
- Gas is then introduced at a controlled velocity that keeps the interface short.
- Vents are monitored until the gas content confirms the purge is complete.

For liquid lines, the product is introduced behind a pig or batching pig train, and the air or nitrogen ahead is vented at the far end. Interfaces between water, nitrogen and product are tracked and directed to slop tanks.

## Preservation

A pipeline is often completed months before it enters service. It must not sit wet. Options:

- Dry it and leave it filled with **dry air or nitrogen** at slight positive pressure, monitored.
- If it must stay water-filled, treat the water with **biocide, oxygen scavenger and inhibitor**, and limit the duration.

<Callout type="warn">
Nitrogen is invisible, odourless and kills quickly by displacing oxygen. Vent points, valve pits and pig trap enclosures during purging are confined-space hazards that need gas monitoring and controlled access.
</Callout>

<Worked title="How much water is left after dewatering?">

**Given:** NPS 24 pipeline, inside diameter 0.585 m, 100 km long. Suppose the pig train leaves a water film averaging 0.05 mm thick on the wall.

**Wall area:** π × 0.585 × 100 000 = 183 800 m².

**Film volume:** 183 800 × 0.00005 = **9.2 m³** of water.

Dry air at −40 °C dew point can absorb roughly 15 to 20 g of water per cubic metre as it warms and saturates at 25 °C. Removing 9200 kg therefore needs of the order of half a million cubic metres of dry air, which is why drying a long line takes days, and why repeated foam pig runs to thin and spread the film matter so much.

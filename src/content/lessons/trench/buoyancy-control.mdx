## Where pipes float

Module M2 showed that a large empty pipe displaces far more water than it weighs. Buoyancy control is needed wherever the trench can fill with water or the soil can behave like a fluid:

- sabkha and coastal flats with a high water table,
- river, wadi and canal crossings,
- marsh, swamp and irrigated farmland,
- flood plains, even if dry at the time of construction,
- soils that can liquefy in an earthquake.

The check is made for the **empty** pipe, with the density of the actual groundwater, to a safety factor of about 1.1 (higher in flowing water).

## How many weights

For intermittent weights, each unit must hold down the length of pipe between it and its neighbours.

$$
\text{spacing} = \frac{\text{net submerged weight of one unit}}{1.1 \times \text{buoyancy} - \text{pipe weight}}
$$

A concrete weight is itself buoyant: in water its effective weight is only about 58 % of its weight in air, and less in brine.

<WeightSpacing client:visible />

Try this: for NPS 30 pipe in sabkha brine, vary the weight mass and watch the spacing. Then switch the fluid to liquefied soil and see why continuous concrete coating takes over.

Spacing is also capped so the pipe cannot lift between weights and overstress in bending. A common practical limit is about one pipe joint (12 m).

## Construction issues

- **Float during construction.** A sudden rainstorm or a failed dewatering pump can float an open-trench pipe string in minutes. Weights go on as the pipe goes in, not later.
- **Coating protection** under every weight or strap.
- **Electrical isolation.** Reinforcing steel and anchor straps must not touch the pipe, or they drain cathodic protection current.
- **Negative buoyancy for installation.** For pulled or floated crossings the pipe weight is tuned so it is slightly heavy, and can be flooded once in position.
- **Hydrotest water.** A water-filled pipe is stable, which can hide a buoyancy problem until dewatering.

<Worked title="Saddle weights for an NPS 30 line in sabkha">

**Given:** OD 762 mm, wall 9.5 mm, empty, brine 1150 kg/m³. From module M2: pipe weight 1.80 kN/m, buoyancy 5.23 kN/m. Weights of 3000 kg, concrete 2400 kg/m³.

**Downforce needed:** 1.1 × 5.23 − 1.80 = **3.95 kN/m**.

**Net weight of one unit in brine:**

$$
3000 \times 9.81 \times \left( 1 - \frac{1150}{2400} \right) = 15.3 \text{ kN}
$$

**Spacing:** 15.3 / 3.95 = **3.9 m**, about three weights per 12 m joint.

At that density of weights, continuous concrete coating of about 85 mm (module M2) is likely to be cheaper and more reliable.

</Worked>

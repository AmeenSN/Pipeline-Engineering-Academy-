## Layers of defence

Third-party damage is the leading cause of pipeline failure. Marking is part of a layered defence:

1. **Awareness.** Land-use planning, public information, one-call and permit-to-dig systems.
2. **Above-ground markers** that tell people a pipeline is there before they dig.
3. **Below-ground warning**: tape or mesh that an excavator finds before the pipe.
4. **Physical protection**: slabs, extra cover, heavier wall.
5. **Detection**: patrols and monitoring that spot activity on the right of way.

No single layer is reliable alone. Each catches some of what the others miss.

## What a marker says

A marker is only useful if it can be read and acted on. It carries:

- a warning word such as **WARNING**, **CAUTION** or **DANGER**,
- the product: gas pipeline, petroleum pipeline, and so on,
- the operator's name,
- a telephone number answered at all times,
- often the kilometre point and a "do not dig before calling" message,
- text in the local language as well as English.

Markers are placed so they are not easily knocked down by vehicles, are visible above vegetation and drifting sand, and are checked and replaced on patrol.

## Marking during construction

Before the permanent markers go in, the construction team marks:

- the surveyed centreline and right-of-way boundaries with stakes,
- **every existing buried service**, with its depth, before trenching reaches it,
- overhead power lines, with height limit goalposts for cranes and sidebooms,
- open trench, with barriers and lights near roads and settlements.

## Records are markers too

The most precise "marker" is the as-built survey. Operators hold pipeline positions in a geographic information system and share them through one-call or permit systems, so that anyone planning to excavate is told what is there. In the field, the line is then located precisely with an electromagnetic pipe locator and marked on the ground before digging starts.
